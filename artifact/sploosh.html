What you’re hearing

Water on its own is almost silent. What we call the sound of water is mostly the sound of air trapped in it.

1 · A bubble is a tiny spring

The air inside a bubble is a spring; the water around it is the mass. Squeezed at birth, it rings at the Minnaert frequency, which depends only on its radius: a 1 mm bubble sings at 3.3 kHz, a 1 cm one at 330 Hz. It rings down within milliseconds, and a bubble near the surface slides up in pitch as it rises. That upward slide is the “plink” you know from dripping taps.

2 · A step tears air into pockets

The foot drags air under the surface and the turbulence tears it into pockets; each pocket splits into a few bubbles of similar size within two milliseconds. Many small ones make the bright fizz, a few big ones make the “oo” in sploosh. Bubbles near the surface are loud and rising; deep ones are quiet and steady. In deeper water the cavity above the foot pinches off into a couple of big bubbles, the “bloop”.

D is how close to the surface a bubble is born (0 = deep, 1 = at the surface). Loud bubbles are rare: D is drawn as u² for u uniform.

3 · What goes up comes down

The sole throws water up. Each blob or droplet flies a little parabola and lands T = 2vz/g later: blobs splat with a few bubbles, fine droplets tick. Drops thrown faster fly longer and land harder, which is what sets the length of the patter after the splash.

4 · The foot itself

First the sole slaps the surface: a few milliseconds of noise as the contact line sweeps across it, plus the cushion of air trapped underneath ringing near 200 Hz. The forefoot lands 25–50 ms later. The heel reaches the ground after roughly h / (0.6 v), muffled by the water above it. Lifting the foot out pulls water in behind the heel (a rising “shlup”), then the shoe drips.

5 · Summing it up

A single step is between one and three thousand of these events. Each is computed sample by sample, spread across left and right, summed, given a touch of outdoor reverb and normalised. Nothing is sampled from a recording: the numbers on this page are the whole instrument.

How it got here

Every version of the synthesizer is kept. Each row below runs that version’s code live on the same ankle-deep water: six steps of wading, or a single step.

Bubble model after K. van den Doel, “Physically based models for liquid sounds”, ACM Transactions on Applied Perception 2(4), 2005: Minnaert resonance, the damping law, and the rising pitch of near-surface bubbles. The splash structure (pockets, blobs, ballistic droplets, the foot) is this project’s own.
